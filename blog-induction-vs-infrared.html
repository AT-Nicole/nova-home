How to Choose an Induction Cooker Manufacturer: The OEM Buyer's Checklist

Verify certifications by model, check in-house production, understand the OEM tiers, and ask the four questions that separate factories from traders.

Three technologies, one shelf space. If you are an importer or distributor deciding what goes into your next container of portable cooking appliances, the choice between induction cookers, infrared cookers, and electric ceramic hobs is not about which is "best" — it is about which market you serve, which cookware your buyers own, and what price point your channel supports.

This guide compares the three technologies side by side, with the market signals we see as a factory shipping to 40+ countries, and ends with a practical selection matrix.

The three technologies in 60 seconds

Induction cooker. Uses an electromagnetic coil under a glass-ceramic surface to heat the pan directly. The surface stays relatively cool; the pan becomes the heating element. Fastest boil times and the best energy efficiency (roughly 85-90% vs ~60-70% for radiant types). Requires ferromagnetic cookware — if a magnet sticks to the pan, it works. This is the dominant portable cooking category in the EU, and demand is spiking in India amid LPG shortages.

Infrared cooker (electric ceramic / halogen). Uses halogen or infrared tubes below the glass-ceramic surface. Works with any flat-bottomed cookware, including glass and ceramic pots. Slower temperature response than induction, but visually dramatic (the tubes glow) and familiar to buyers upgrading from traditional hotplates. Popular in the Middle East and parts of Southeast Asia, and a strong budget-to-mid range option.

Electric ceramic hob. A radiant coil or ribbon element heating a glass-ceramic top. Glows red-orange when hot, works with nearly any cookware, and has a lower price point. The trade-off: more residual heat, slower response, and higher energy consumption. Still the workhorse in price-sensitive markets and hospitality back-of-house applications.

What the order data says (from a factory that ships all three)

  • EU buyers order induction almost exclusively. Compliance (CE/EMC/LVD/UKCA), efficiency labeling (ErP), and consumer preference all point the same way. If your market is Europe, induction is not optional.
  • The Middle East mixes infrared and induction. Infrared's cookware flexibility matters in households that use traditional pots, while induction is growing in modern kitchens. Distributors often carry both lines.
  • India is an induction story right now. LPG price shocks pushed induction cooker sales up sharply; buyers want 220V wide-voltage models that tolerate grid fluctuation, plus local certification support where applicable.
  • HORECA and price-sensitive channels still buy ceramic. Hotels, canteens, and budget retail keep the ceramic category alive — at a lower margin but with predictable volume.

Four questions to ask your supplier before ordering

  1. Certification per model — induction for the EU needs RoHS + EMC + LVD (EN 60335-2-9) plus UKCA for the UK; confirm the certificate lists your exact models.
  2. Voltage and plug — 220-240V / 110V variants with EU, UK, US or AU plugs; universal voltage on key SKUs.
  3. MOQ and lead time — a sane starting point is 500 pcs per model with 35-40 days after deposit; avoid suppliers whose MOQ shifts between email and quotation.
  4. Sample before container — always. Test the model in your market's actual cooking conditions, with your market's actual pots.

The short version

Induction is the category with the strongest growth and the cleanest compliance story; infrared is the flexible mid-range workhorse; ceramic remains the volume play for price-sensitive channels. The best importers do not pick one technology — they pick a mix that matches their market, and they find one factory that can build all three on the same quality system.
